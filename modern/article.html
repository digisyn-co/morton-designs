Is Connecticut a 50/50 divorce state?

No. Connecticut divides property equitably rather than equally — and it is one of the few states where the court can reach an asset you owned before the marriage or inherited during it.

It is the first thing people ask, and the answer is no. Connecticut is an equitable distribution state, not a community property state, and the difference is not academic — it changes what is on the table before anyone argues about how to split it.

01  Equitable is not equal

A community property state starts from a presumption that marital property is divided down the middle. Connecticut does not. Under C.G.S. §46b-81 the court assigns property between the parties on the statutory factors, and an even split is a possible outcome rather than a starting point.

In practice that means two couples with identical balance sheets can end up with different divisions, because the factors that drive the result — length of the marriage, each party's earnings and earning capacity, health, contributions to the estate — are not identical.

02  The all-property rule

This is the part that surprises people, and it is where Connecticut differs from most of the country. There is no separate category of property that sits outside the court's reach. Assets you owned before the marriage, assets you inherited, assets that were a gift to you alone — the court may assign any of them.

In Connecticut, “that was mine before we married” is an argument about weight, not about jurisdiction.

The origin of an asset still matters. It is one of the things the court weighs, and a recently inherited account that was never commingled is a different argument from one that funded the family home twenty years ago. But it is a factor, not a wall.

03  What the court weighs

Section 46b-81 sets out the factors: the length of the marriage, the causes of the dissolution, the age, health, station, occupation, amount and sources of income, earning capacity, vocational skills, education, employability, estate, liabilities and needs of each party, and the opportunity of each for future acquisition of assets and income. The court also weighs each party's contribution to the acquisition, preservation or appreciation of the estate.

Fault can bear on those factors without dictating the result. Alimony is a separate question again, governed by C.G.S. §46b-82 — and Connecticut has no alimony formula at all, which is a source of a great deal of misplaced confidence on both sides of a case.

04  What you can do about it

Before a marriage, a prenuptial agreement under the Connecticut Premarital Agreement Act is the direct answer to the all-property rule. After one, a postnuptial agreement is possible too, though Connecticut recognizes those by case law rather than statute and applies a stricter fair-and-equitable standard.

Inside a dissolution, the work is evidentiary: establishing what an asset is, where it came from, whether it was kept separate, and what each party actually contributed. That is done with documents, not adjectives, which is why the financial affidavit is the center of a Connecticut case.
